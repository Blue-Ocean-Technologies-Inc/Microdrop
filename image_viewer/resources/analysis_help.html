ROI analysis: what every term means

This guide explains, in plain language, every setting and every number in MicroDrop's image analysis: the Image Viewer pane and the ROI Intensities pane.

You do not need any image-processing background. Each item says what it is, when you would use it, what changing it does, and the value MicroDrop starts with. The small pictures are live: drag the sliders.

How a number is made

Every point on the plot is one number, for one region, in one image. It is made in a fixed order. The first three steps change the picture itself, so they change what is measured. Everything after the measurement only changes how the numbers are shown or compared.

Quick walkthrough

From a folder of images to a curve in six steps. The pictures are sketches of the real controls, not screenshots; the Toolbar icons section shows every button exactly as it looks.

  1. 1Load an image folder

    In the Image Viewer, the folder button Choose the image folder opens the experiment's raw captures by default; pick another experiment under Experiments in the sidebar.

    You should see the first image and its position next to the buttons (for example 7/30). Step with the arrow buttons, or press play to flick through them and check the droplets stay in view.

  2. 2Draw an ROI and name it

    Choose a shape on the toolbar at the image's right edge (the circle is the usual one) and click-drag from the droplet's centre outwards. The tool stays armed, so draw one ROI per droplet, then press Esc.

    Each ROI gets a row in the table under the plot. Double-click its name ("ROI 1") to call it something meaningful; that name is used in the legend and the CSV.

  3. 3Set the background ring

    Open Advanced under the image. In the Measurement box, Ring Gap (start: 2 px) and Ring Width (start: 4 px) place the ring that reads the "nothing" level next to each droplet.

    Turn on Show the background rings (crop icon) to see them. The ring should sit on empty chip, clear of the droplet's glow and of its neighbours.

  4. 4Optional: rolling-ball correction

    If the light is brighter on one side of the chip, switch on the rolling ball (half-filled circle icon) and set Ball Radius.

    The image now shows the flattened picture, which is what gets measured. The target icon below it draws the ball: it should be clearly bigger than your droplets.

  5. 5Calculate and read the plot

    Click Calculate and plot (the chart icon above the image). MicroDrop measures every ROI in every included image and draws the result in the ROI Intensities pane.

    With View on Intensity and Plot on Mean intensity you get one curve per droplet over time. Switch Plot to Background-corrected mean to use the ring from step 3.

  6. 6Clean up, fit and export

    On the Cleanup tab, Outliers drops one-off glitches; on the Fit tab, pick a fit such as Sigmoid to get the dashed model curve and its timing.

    When it looks right, the save icon above the image (Export the intensities) writes a CSV into the experiment's analysis folder; the save icon in the plot pane saves the figure itself.

Regions and images

Before anything can be measured you mark where to look, and decide which images count.

ROI (region of interest) and its shapes Image Viewer, right-hand toolbar

An ROI is an outline you draw on the image around something you want to measure, usually a droplet. MicroDrop reads the brightness of every pixel inside the outline, in every image of the series.

What it is

A shape: ellipse (circle or oval), box (rectangle, corners can be rounded), capsule (a pill shape, good for stretched droplets), or contour (any outline you click point by point).

When to use it

Pick the shape that hugs your droplet best. A tight fit matters: pixels of dark background inside the outline pull the average down.

What changing it does

Moving, resizing or reshaping an ROI changes which pixels are measured, so its numbers are recalculated. Each draw tool stays armed so you can draw several in a row; press Esc to put it away.

Typical value

Slightly smaller than the droplet, so the edge (where the droplet fades into the background) is not included.

Moving an ROI on a later image (drift override) Edit mode

What it is

Droplets can creep across the chip during an experiment. If you edit an ROI while looking at a later image, MicroDrop does not move it everywhere. It records a change that applies from that image onward. Earlier images keep the old position.

When to use it

When a droplet has moved partly out of its outline. Step to the first image where it has moved and drag the ROI back onto it.

What changing it does

Only the images from that point on are re-measured. Reset can also clear these overrides. The AI Track button creates them automatically.

Typical value

None until you need one.

AI detection options AI Detection box (when AI support is installed)

The AI tools find droplets for you. Pick outlines the droplet you click, Detect all searches the whole image, and Track follows your ROIs through later images. What it finds appears as dashed candidates: click one to discard it, then press Accept.

Significance

Detect all asks the AI at many points in a grid. Significance is how many of those points must independently find the same droplet. Clear droplets usually score 2 to 4; a one-off speck scores 1. Raise it to hide noise, lower it to see faint droplets. Nothing is deleted: sliding it back brings candidates back. Starts at 2. Candidates you clicked yourself are never hidden by it.

Min Size / Max Size

Hide candidates smaller or larger than this average diameter, in pixels. Use them to throw away dust (too small) or whole electrodes (too big). Start at 0 and 500 px.

Output Shape

What an accepted candidate becomes: the traced polygon outline (follows the droplet exactly) or a fitted ellipse (smoother, easier to edit). Starts as polygon.

Drift Interval

While tracking, re-find the droplets every Nth image (the last image is always checked); images in between keep the last position. A larger number tracks faster but follows quick movement less closely. Starts at 3.

Excluding images Analysis Exclusion box

What it is

A mark that tells the analysis to skip an image: it is left out of the calculation, the plot, the CSV and drift tracking. You can still view it and draw on it. Before/After buttons mark or unmark every image on one side of the one shown.

When to use it

A frame that is blurred, over-exposed, or was taken while something was being adjusted.

What changing it does

An excluded image simply disappears from the series. It does not leave a gap in the line. The mark is saved with the experiment.

Typical value

Nothing excluded.

Image scale (calibration) Ruler button

What it is

Tells MicroDrop how big a pixel is in real life. Drag a line over something whose length you know (for example an electrode of known width), then type that length and unit.

When to use it

When you want areas in mm² or µm² instead of pixels, or a scale bar on the image.

What changing it does

It does not change which pixels are measured. It changes the Area column and the Per area values, which are expressed in your unit instead of px².

Typical value

Not calibrated: sizes are reported in pixels (px²).

Contrast (Auto contrast, Min, Max) Contrast box

What it is

How the image is displayed: which brightness shows as black and which as white. Camera images often hold far more brightness levels than a screen can show, and look almost black without this.

When to use it

To see faint droplets more clearly, or to compare images with the same display settings.

What changing it does

Display only. It never changes a measured number.

Typical value

Auto contrast on: the darkest 0.1% of pixels show as black and the brightest 0.1% as white.

Measurement settings

These live in the Measurement box under the image. They change what is measured, so changing one makes MicroDrop recalculate. The settings used are written into every row of the CSV export.

Background ring (Ring Gap, Ring Width) Measurement box; crop button shows it

A droplet's brightness is partly the droplet and partly the chip, light leaking around, and camera offset. To tell them apart, MicroDrop measures a thin ring of chip just outside each ROI. The ring tells you how bright "nothing" is right next to that droplet.

What it is

A band (in maths, an annulus) that follows the ROI's outline at a small distance. Ring Gap is the empty space between the ROI edge and the ring. Ring Width is how thick the ring is. Both are in pixels. It works for any shape, not just circles.

When to use it

Whenever you plot a background-corrected value. It is especially useful when the light is uneven across the chip, because each droplet gets its own local background instead of one value for the whole picture.

What changing it does

Gap too small: the ring catches the droplet's glow, so "background" reads too bright and the corrected value too low.

Gap too large: the ring drifts away from the droplet and may reach a different-looking part of the chip.

Width too thin: few pixels, so the background value is noisy. Too wide: it reaches further away and into neighbours (other ROIs are always cut out of it).

Changing either value recalculates every number.

Typical value

Gap 2 px, Width 4 px (Gap can be 0 to 50, Width 1 to 50).

Rolling-ball background correction (Ball Radius) Measurement box; tonality button turns it on

Microscope and camera light is rarely even: often the middle of the picture is brighter than the corners. A droplet in the bright middle would then look brighter than an identical one in a corner. The rolling ball removes that slow, gentle unevenness from the whole image before anything is measured.

What it is

Picture the brightness of the image as a landscape: bright places are hills. Droplets are narrow, steep hills; uneven lighting is a broad, gentle rise underneath them.

Now push a ball up into that landscape from below, everywhere. Where the ball is too wide to fit into a narrow droplet-hill, it stops at the hill's foot. The surface the top of the ball reaches is the background. MicroDrop subtracts it, which leaves the droplets standing on a flat floor.

(The name comes from the classic ImageJ tool. In MicroDrop the "ball" is really a flat disk, like a coin pushed up from underneath, which is why the demo draws a flat bar. The idea is identical.)

When to use it

When droplets in different parts of the picture should be comparable, but the illumination is brighter in some places. While it is on, the image you see is the corrected one, so what you see is what is measured. The adjust button next to it draws the ball on the image at its real size; drag its grip to choose the radius by eye.

What changing it does

Radius too small (smaller than your droplets): the ball climbs into the droplets and counts part of them as background. Your signal shrinks or disappears. Try 20 px in the demo.

Radius too large: the ball cannot follow the gentle unevenness either, so some of it stays. Try 180 px.

Rule of thumb: comfortably larger than your biggest droplet's radius, but smaller than the size of the lighting pattern. Every number is recalculated when you change it, and the ring is measured on the corrected image too.

Typical value

Off by default. When on, it starts at 50 px (allowed range 5 to 500 px).

Perspective correction 3D-view button defines it; transform button applies it

What it is

If the camera looks at the chip slightly from an angle, rectangles look like trapezoids. You click four points on the image (for example the chip's corners), then drag them to where they should be. MicroDrop then straightens every image the same way.

When to use it

When the chip appears tilted or skewed, so that ROIs drawn on one part of it are distorted.

What changing it does

When applied, every image is straightened first, before the rolling ball and before measuring. Your ROIs are drawn on, and measured on, the straightened image. Turning it on or off recalculates everything.

Typical value

Not defined. Once defined, you can switch it on and off.

What you can plot (the Y axis)

The Plot list above the chart chooses which number is drawn for each ROI. "Intensity" is how bright a pixel is, as a count from the camera. A brighter fluorescent droplet gives bigger counts.

Mean intensity

What it is

The average brightness of all pixels inside the ROI.

When to use it

The everyday choice: "how bright is this droplet?" It does not depend on how big you drew the ROI, as long as it sits inside the droplet.

How to read it

Includes the background glow of the chip underneath, so two droplets on differently lit parts of the chip are not directly comparable. For that, use the background-corrected mean.

Typical value

This is the default plot.

Background-corrected mean

What it is

The mean inside the ROI minus the mean of its background ring. In words: how much brighter the droplet is than the chip right next to it.

When to use it

The standard fluorescence measurement. Use it when the background varies across the chip or changes over time.

How to read it

Zero means "no brighter than its surroundings". It can go slightly negative for an empty spot. It depends on the ring's Gap and Width.

Typical value

Ring Gap 2 px, Width 4 px.

Median intensity

What it is

Line all the ROI's pixels up from darkest to brightest and take the one in the middle.

When to use it

When a few pixels are wildly off (a dust speck, a hot pixel, a reflection). The median ignores them; the mean does not.

How to read it

Close to the mean for an evenly lit droplet. If the two differ a lot, something in the ROI is uneven.

Typical value

Not background-corrected.

Min intensity and Max intensity

What it is

The darkest and the brightest single pixel inside the ROI.

When to use it

Checking problems rather than measuring. Max tells you whether the camera is saturated: if it sits at the camera's top value (for example 65535 on a 16-bit camera), the droplet is too bright to measure correctly. Min shows whether your ROI reaches into dark background.

How to read it

Single pixels are noisy, so these lines jump around more than the mean.

Typical value

Not background-corrected.

Outline ring mean

What it is

The average brightness of the background ring alone: the "nothing" level next to the droplet.

When to use it

To check the background itself. If it rises over time (for example, the chip starts to glow when it heats), you know the background correction is doing real work.

How to read it

It should be flat and low. If it follows the droplet's curve, the ring is probably catching the droplet's glow: increase Ring Gap.

Typical value

Measured with Gap 2 px, Width 4 px.

Integrated and Integrated (bg-corrected)

What it is

The total light in the ROI: the mean multiplied by the number of pixels. The bg-corrected version uses the background-corrected mean, so it is the total light above background.

When to use it

When the droplet's amount of fluorescent material matters, not its brightness. A droplet that spreads out gets dimmer, but its total stays the same.

How to read it

It grows with the ROI's size. Two ROIs of different sizes on the same droplet give different totals, so draw ROIs that cover the whole droplet when you use it. Units are counts × pixels, so the numbers are large.

Typical value

Pick it from the Plot list.

Per area and Per area (bg-corrected)

What it is

Brightness per unit of real area: the mean divided by the area of one pixel. With a calibrated image scale it reads "counts per mm²" (or µm²); without one, "per px²", which is the same as the mean.

When to use it

To compare experiments taken at different magnifications or with different cameras, where one pixel covers a different area of chip.

How to read it

Within one experiment its curve has exactly the same shape as the mean (it is the mean times a fixed number). It does not depend on how big the ROI is.

Typical value

Only meaningful once the image scale is set.

Table and CSV-only columns (Count, Area, std, outline_*)

What it is

Count: how many pixels are inside the ROI. Area: count × the area of one pixel, in your calibrated unit (px² if not calibrated).

The CSV export also has std (standard deviation: how much the pixels vary around their mean) and the same stats for the ring, prefixed outline_.

When to use it

Count and Area confirm the ROI size; std tells you how even the droplet is. The table under the plot shows these for the image currently displayed.

How to read it

The CSV is one row per image per ROI. It also records the outlier flag and the ring, ball and perspective settings used, so a file always says how it was made.

Typical value

Not plotted.

Cleanup tab

These options tidy the curves. None of them changes your stored measurements; switch them on and off freely.

The order things happen

When several options are on, they are applied in this order, so each one sees what it should:

  1. Outliers are removed first, so one bad point cannot spoil the steps after it.
  2. Bkg Ref subtraction.
  3. Hidden ROIs (eye off) are dropped from the figure.
  4. Subtract first.
  5. Normalize.

Fits use the result of these steps. Smoothing and Bridge gaps are applied only to the drawn line, after fitting.

Outliers (MADs, win)

What it is

An automatic check for single bad points, such as a frame where a bubble passed or the light flickered. For each point it looks at its neighbours, finds their median (the middle value), and how spread out they normally are (the MAD, median absolute deviation). A point much further from the median than usual is dropped.

Because it uses medians rather than averages, the spike cannot raise the very bar it is measured against. (Its formal name is the Hampel test.)

When to use it

When a few isolated points jump away from an otherwise smooth curve and spoil a fit.

What changing it does

MADs: how far is "too far". 3 means roughly "three times the normal scatter", like "3 sigma". Lower drops more points (risking real data); higher drops only extreme ones.

win: how many points, in total, each point is judged against (5 = the point and two on each side). Wider catches clusters of bad points, but if it spans a real, fast change it may start calling real data outliers.

Dropped points become gaps. The plot counts them ("N points dropped as outliers"), the CSV marks them in its outlier column, and fits ignore them.

Typical value

Off. When on: MADs 3 (1 to 20) and win 5 (3 to 51).

Bridge gaps

What it is

Where a point is missing (an image not calculated yet, or a dropped outlier), the line normally breaks. With Bridge gaps on, it is drawn straight across instead.

When to use it

For a cleaner-looking figure. Turn it off when you want to see exactly where data is missing.

What changing it does

Display only. The fits and the CSV keep the gaps; no invented values are stored.

Typical value

On.

Smoothing (Savitzky-Golay, Butterworth)

What it is

Draws a calmer line through noisy points. Two methods:

Savitzky-Golay: for each point, fit a small curve through it and its neighbours and use that curve's value. win is how many points each little curve uses (always odd); order is how bendy the little curve may be (1 = straight line, 2 = simple arc).

Butterworth: a classic "low-pass" filter that keeps slow changes and removes fast jitter. order is how sharply it separates the two; cutoff sets where "slow" ends, as a fraction of the fastest change the points can show (0.2 keeps only changes slower than about a fifth of that). It runs forwards and backwards, so peaks do not shift in time.

When to use it

For a readable figure when points are noisy. Savitzky-Golay keeps peaks and steps sharper; Butterworth gives a very smooth line.

What changing it does

Display only. Fits always use the unsmoothed points: smoothing makes neighbouring points depend on each other, which would make a fit look better (higher R²) than it really is. Gaps stay gaps. Smoothing works on point spacing, not seconds.

Typical value

No smoothing. Savitzky-Golay starts at win 7, order 2 (win 3 to 101, order 1 to 6). Butterworth starts at order 2, cutoff 0.2 (order 1 to 8, cutoff 0.01 to 0.99).

Normalize

What it is

Stretches each ROI's curve so its own lowest point is 0% and its highest is 100%.

When to use it

To compare the shape and timing of curves whose brightness differs a lot: "which droplet reacted first?"

What changing it does

The actual brightness is lost from the figure, and the Y axis reads "% of range". A flat curve sits at 0%. Fits run on the normalised values.

Typical value

Off.

Subtract first

What it is

Takes each curve's first value away from all its values, so every curve starts at zero.

When to use it

When you care about the change from the start, not the starting level. Droplets that started at different brightness can then be compared directly.

What changing it does

Shifts each curve up or down; its shape is unchanged. The Y axis says "(change from first)". If the first point is missing, the first available one is used.

Typical value

Off.

Bkg Ref (background reference)

What it is

You draw an ROI over a patch of chip with no sample in it and tick Bkg Ref for it in the table. With the Bkg Ref option on, that patch's value is subtracted from every ROI, image by image. If you tick several, their average is used.

When to use it

When the background changes over time for the whole chip at once: lamp drift, the chip glowing as it heats, camera warming up. The ring corrects each droplet locally; Bkg Ref corrects for what the whole image does over time. They can be used together.

What changing it does

The Y axis says "(bkg-ref corrected)". It still works if you hide the reference ROI with its eye. If no ROI is ticked, nothing is subtracted.

Typical value

Off, and no ROI ticked.

Fit tab and views

A fit draws the best-matching smooth equation through each ROI's points, so you can read off numbers such as the midpoint of a melt curve or the speed of a decay.

When to use it

Pick the shape your process should have. For a DNA melt, the sigmoid's midpoint is the melting temperature (with the X axis set to temperature).

What changing it does

Each ROI gets its own fit, drawn dashed in its colour. The fit uses the cleaned-up points (after outliers, Bkg Ref, Subtract first and Normalize) but never the smoothed line. Each method needs a minimum number of points (2 for linear up to 5 for sigmoid); with fewer, no curve is drawn.

Typical value

No fit.

R² (goodness of fit)

What it is

A score of how well the fitted curve passes through the points. 1 means perfectly; 0 means no better than a flat line at the average.

When to use it

Shown in the fitted-equations table. Use it to judge whether you can trust the fitted numbers.

How to read it

Above about 0.99 is very good for a clean fluorescence curve. A low R² means the shape is wrong for the data, or something (like a fading tail) is disturbing it.

Typical value

Trim tail aims for 0.99.

Custom equations, presets and start values function button opens the equations window

What it is

In the equations window you can type your own equation in x, for example a + b*exp(-c*x). Every other name (a, b, c) is a number MicroDrop finds for you. The table lists every ROI's fitted numbers and R². You can save an equation as a preset so it appears in the Fit list for all experiments.

When to use it

When none of the built-in shapes describes your process.

What changing it does

Start values give the solver a starting guess for each number. Leave them empty and it tries several guesses itself. If your guesses lead nowhere, it falls back to its own and says so. "Seed from fit" copies the last answer in as the new guesses.

Typical value

Empty start values (automatic).

Trim tail

What it is

If the fit is poor (R² below 0.99), MicroDrop tries again on a shorter series, dropping points from the end (about 10% at a time, keeping at least 10 points), until a fit reaches 0.99. The dropped end is shaded grey.

When to use it

When the end of the experiment no longer follows the model, for example the dye bleaches after the curve has levelled off. That fading tail can drag a sigmoid's midpoint to the wrong place.

What changing it does

The fitted equation then describes only the unshaded part; the equation text says "fit to t ≤ …". If no shorter slice reaches 0.99, the full fit is kept.

Typical value

Off.

Equations

What it is

Writes each ROI's fitted equation, with its numbers filled in, into the corner of the figure.

When to use it

For a saved figure that should carry its fit results.

What changing it does

Display only. The equations table (function button) shows the same numbers in a grid.

Typical value

Off. Available once a fit method is chosen.

d² max, d² min, and V-line / H-line / Coords Fit tab, Advanced metrics

What it is

The first derivative is how fast the curve is changing. The second derivative (d²) is how fast that speed is changing: it shows where the curve bends.

d² max marks where the curve bends upward most: on a rising S-curve, where it takes off. d² min marks where it bends downward most: where it starts to level off. Both are taken from the fitted curve, not the raw points.

When to use it

To define "onset" and "end" of a reaction objectively, for example the start of melting, or a threshold time in amplification.

What changing it does

V-line drops a vertical line through each marker (read the time or temperature off the X axis), H-line runs a horizontal one (read the value), and Coords writes the (x, y) numbers next to it. Straight lines and quadratics have no single bend point, so nothing is marked for them.

Typical value

Markers off. When on: V-line on, H-line off, Coords on.

View: Intensity, 2nd derivative, Fastest change View list above the chart

What it is

Intensity: the normal chart of your chosen stat.

2nd derivative: draws each fit's d² curve (the lower panel in the demo above). Peaks and troughs are the d² max and min.

Fastest change: one bar per ROI showing when its fitted curve changed fastest. For a sigmoid that is its midpoint.

When to use it

Fastest change is a quick way to compare timing across many droplets: "which reacted first, and by how much?"

What changing it does

The two extra views need a fit method; without one they show a hint instead. A straight-line fit changes at a constant speed, so it has no "fastest" moment and no bar.

Typical value

Intensity.

Axes, styling and export

Auto / Min / Max / Log Axes tab

What it is

One row per axis. Auto fits the axis to the data. Untick it to type your own Min and Max. Log spaces the axis by multiples (1, 10, 100…) instead of equal steps.

When to use it

Fixed limits make several saved figures directly comparable. A log axis helps when values span a huge range, or to check whether a decay is exponential (it becomes a straight line on a log Y axis).

What changing it does

Display only; data and fits are unchanged. Zero and negative values cannot be drawn on a log axis; they are counted in a note on the figure. You can also scroll to zoom and drag to pan the chart.

Typical value

Auto on, Log off. Legend (next to the X axis list) is on.

X axis: Time or Temperature

What it is

Time (s): seconds since the first image shown. Temperature (°C): the heater's temperature at the moment each image was taken, read from the heater's log files.

When to use it

Temperature for melt curves and anything driven by heating; time for everything else.

What changing it does

The fits follow the X axis, so a sigmoid's midpoint comes out in °C instead of seconds. Images the heater log does not cover cannot be placed; the figure counts them ("N frames outside the heater log's coverage").

Typical value

Time.

Heater logs, Sensor, Window (ms) Axes tab, shown when X axis is Temperature

Heater logs

The folder holding the heater's .jsonl log files. It defaults to the experiment's own heater_logs folder; use the folder button to pick another. Logs and images are matched by the clock time each was recorded.

Sensor

Which temperature sensor to use. mean (the default) averages all sensors on each reading. Pick a single sensor if it sits nearest your droplets.

Window (ms)

How generous the time match is. Each image gets the average of all heater readings within ± half the window of its capture time. 0 (the default) means exact: the temperature is worked out at the precise capture moment from the readings either side. If a window holds no reading at all, that image becomes a gap rather than a guess. Range 0 to 300 000 ms.

When to use it

Leave Window at 0 unless the readings are noisy (a window then averages them) or the heater and camera clocks do not line up exactly.

Per-ROI styling and visibility Table under the plot

What it is

Each row of the table is one ROI. You can rename it, and set its eye (shown or hidden), Alpha (opacity, 0 to 100%), Color, Line style, Marker shape and marker Size. Bkg Ref marks it as a background reference.

When to use it

To make a figure readable, or to hide reference ROIs once they have done their job.

What changing it does

Display only. A hidden ROI is still measured, listed in the table and exported to the CSV.

Typical value

Visible, fully opaque, solid line, no markers, colours assigned in turn.

Export (DPI, Format), Calculate, Reset and CSV

What it is

Calculate (chart button in the Image Viewer) measures every ROI in every shown image that has not been measured yet. Reset throws away the stored measurements (and optionally the drift overrides) so they are recalculated. Export writes the CSV to the experiment's analysis folder, calculating first if needed.

The save button above the chart saves the figure as an image, at the Export tab's DPI (dots per inch: sharpness when printed) and Format.

When to use it

PNG for slides and documents, SVG or PDF for figures you will edit or scale, TIFF where a journal asks for it.

What changing it does

Higher DPI gives a larger, sharper file.

Typical value

300 DPI, PNG (also 150 or 600 DPI; SVG, PDF, TIFF).

Glossary

Pixel
One dot of the camera image. Each holds one brightness number.
Intensity, counts
A pixel's brightness as the camera reports it. Bigger is brighter. A 16-bit camera counts from 0 to 65535.
ROI
Region of interest: an outline you draw around what you want to measure.
Background
Light that is not from your sample: chip glow, stray light, camera offset.
Halo, glow
The droplet's light spilling a pixel or two past its edge. It is signal, not background.
Annulus
A ring shape, like a washer. The background ring is one.
Mean, median
Mean: the average. Median: the middle value when sorted; not thrown off by a few extreme values.
Standard deviation (std)
How much values typically scatter around their mean.
MAD
Median absolute deviation: the typical distance of values from their median. A scatter measure that ignores extreme values.
Outlier
A single point far from its neighbours, usually caused by a glitch rather than the sample.
Saturated
A pixel at the camera's maximum count. The true brightness is higher but cannot be measured.
Fit, parameter
A fit finds the numbers (parameters) that make an equation pass as close as possible to your points.
R²
Fit quality from 0 to 1; 1 is perfect.
Sigmoid
An S-shaped curve going from one level to another. Its midpoint is where it is halfway and changing fastest.
Derivative
The rate of change: how steep the curve is. The second derivative (d²) is how quickly that steepness changes, which shows where the curve bends.
Normalize
Rescale to 0–100% of the curve's own range.
Baseline
The starting level a change is measured from.
Interpolate
Estimate a value between two known ones by drawing a straight line between them.
Low-pass filter
A smoother that keeps slow changes and removes fast jitter (Butterworth is one).
Nyquist frequency
The fastest change a series of points can show (an up-down every two points). Butterworth's cutoff is a fraction of it.
Rolling ball
A way to estimate uneven background by pushing a disk up under the brightness landscape. Things narrower than the disk count as signal.
Perspective
The distortion when a camera looks at a flat surface at an angle.
Calibration
Telling the software how long one pixel is in real units.
px², mm², µm²
Units of area: square pixels, square millimetres, square micrometres.
Thermistor, sensor
The heater's temperature probes.
DPI
Dots per inch: how sharp a saved figure is when printed.
Log axis
An axis where each step multiplies (1, 10, 100) instead of adds.
