Getting Started with MicroDrop

From a freshly unpacked DropBot to your first protocol: connect, line the camera up with the chip, load liquid, make and move droplets, and then write those moves down as a protocol that MicroDrop runs for you.

The clips and photos are real recordings of a DropBot chip. Where you can try things out, for routes and protocols, the pictures are simulations on a photo of the real chip: press ▶ Play to watch, or Step ▸ to go one move at a time.

1. Set up and connect

Where things are, and how to get MicroDrop talking to the DropBot.

  1. Device Viewer. The live camera picture of the chip, with every electrode outlined on top. You click electrodes here to switch them on and draw routes for droplets to follow.
  2. Side panel. Camera, alignment, route settings and more, in collapsible sections. The strip between the picture and the panel hides or shows it.
  3. Mode buttons at the bottom of the side panel: what a click or drag on the chip does.
  4. Dropbot Status. Is the DropBot connected, is a chip in, voltage, frequency and live readings.
  5. Pluggable Protocol Tree. Your protocol: a list of steps, with the buttons that run it.
  6. Status bar. Says whether you are in Free Mode (trying things out) or Editing a protocol step.

Connect

  1. Plug in the DropBot's power and USB cable, then start MicroDrop.
  2. Insert a chip. In Dropbot Status you should see Connection: Active and Chip Status: Present.
  3. Click Realtime Off so it reads Realtime On (or press T with the Device Viewer selected). Only now does the DropBot actually power the electrodes you click.
  4. Set the voltage and frequency in the boxes on the right of the pane. The exact numbers depend on your chip and liquid; ask whoever supplied the chip, or start with the values MicroDrop opens with.
More on this pane: Help ▸ Tutorials ▸ Dropbot Status & Controls Tutorial explains every field, the realtime switch and the capacitance calibration.

2. Line up the camera with the chip

MicroDrop draws the electrode outlines over the camera picture. Aligning tells it where the chip sits in the picture, so the outlines land on the real electrodes and you can click right on the liquid. Do it once per chip holder and camera position; MicroDrop remembers it.

  1. Open Camera Controls in the side panel. Pick your Camera and Resolution, and switch the camera on with .
  2. Click Align Camera. The Camera Alignment window opens: Device Outline, a still frame from the camera, on the left, and Endpoint, the chip drawing, on the right.
  3. On the left, click (①) to show every corner the dots can snap onto, then drag each of the four orange dots onto a corner of the electrode area. They snap onto those corners. Scroll to zoom in for precision, drag the picture to pan.
  4. Make the frame and the drawing face the same way. Each pane has its own Rotate a quarter turn clockwise (②), and the frame has two mirror buttons (③); in the clip the drawing is turned first, then the frame. (④) grabs a fresh frame.
  5. On the right, drag the drawing's four dots onto the same four corners, in the same order: the dots are numbered, and dot 1 must sit on the same corner in both panes. If the order differs, MicroDrop asks before going on.
  6. Click Confirm Alignment (⑦) and close the window. MicroDrop saves the endpoint and the camera picture glides into place under the electrode outlines.
  7. Check: the outlines should sit on the real electrode edges everywhere. If they drift, open Align Camera again and nudge the dots. After moving the camera, Go To Endpoint re-applies the saved frame.
To turn the whole view, use Rotate Device in Camera Controls (R). The pictures in this tutorial show the chip the way MicroDrop shows it: the eight large reservoirs on the left and right, and the grid of small electrodes running down the middle.

Capacitance calibration (optional, recommended)

The Calibration section at the bottom of the side panel teaches MicroDrop what the filler and your liquid look like electrically, which powers the volume and force readings.

  1. Switch on one electrode that has only filler on it, next to a droplet. Click Measure filler Capacitance (①). Cf shows the reading in pF/mm².
  2. Move the droplet onto that electrode (switch it on next door, then switch off where it was, as in chapter 5).
  3. Click Measure Liquid Capacitance (②). Cl shows the reading.
  4. In Dropbot Status, open Pick a dielectric... and choose your chip's coating. MicroDrop then shows the coating's thickness next to Dielectric, and the force reading uses the calibration.

A measurement uses the electrodes that are switched on, so switch on exactly the one you mean. The Dropbot Status tutorial covers this in detail.

3. Load a reservoir

The large electrodes at the sides are reservoirs. Liquid goes in at the edge of the glass top plate, not on top of the plate and not into the middle of the chip.

  1. Make sure Realtime On is showing, and you are in Mode: Draw.
  2. Find the reservoir you want, for example 26 on the left (the photos show its neighbour 34, which works the same). Its outer end sticks out past the glass top plate: that strip is where you load.
  3. Click the reservoir to switch it on. It turns darker blue-grey.
  4. With a pipette, place a small drop (about 2 µL) on the reservoir, right against the edge of the glass. The drop should touch the glass edge.
  5. The switched-on reservoir pulls the liquid in under the plate. It fills the reservoir pad from the glass edge inwards and takes its shape: a U around electrode 20, the first neck electrode, which sits in a notch of the reservoir. The liquid stays off 20 until you switch 20 on.
  6. To add more, pipette again at the same spot while the reservoir is still on.
Common mistakes: pipetting on top of the glass (nothing happens), pipetting with the reservoir off (the drop just sits at the edge), and pipetting too far from the edge (the drop never touches the gap, so it is not pulled in).

4. Dispense a droplet by hand

A droplet is pulled out of the reservoir along the row of electrodes leading from it, then cut off. Each click switches one electrode on or off, and with Realtime on the DropBot follows straight away.

  1. Keep the reservoir (26) on.
  2. Click the next electrodes out of the reservoir one at a time: 20, 21, 22 and 16. Wait for the liquid to reach each one before clicking the next. A finger of liquid follows, and the liquid in the reservoir goes down by exactly what the finger takes.
  3. Click 20 and 21 again to switch them off. Leave the reservoir and the last two (22, 16) on.
  4. The finger thins in the middle and snaps. A droplet stays on 22 and 16; the rest runs back into the reservoir.
Hover over an electrode to see its number (Electrode ID, Channel and area). The pictures here label electrodes by channel number, the same number MicroDrop shows.

5. Move a droplet by clicking

A droplet moves onto a switched-on electrode next to it. Switch on the electrode in front, then switch off the one behind.

  1. The droplet sits on 22 and 16. Click 103 (to its right) to switch it on: the droplet stretches onto it. It gains no liquid, so it gets thinner as it spreads over three electrodes.
  2. Click 22 to switch it off: the droplet lets go of it and now sits on 16 and 103. It has moved one electrode to the right.
  3. Repeat to keep going. Always move to a neighbour; a droplet cannot jump over an electrode.

How much liquid is that?

The Volume readout in Dropbot Status estimates the liquid on the electrodes that are switched on, from the capacitance and the calibration of chapter 2. Its menu switches between Volume, Fill and Covered area; the pop-out button beside it, Show all droplet readouts, opens a small Droplet Readouts window that shows them all and stays open while you work.

6. Routes: let MicroDrop do the clicking

A route is a line drawn across the electrodes. When it runs, MicroDrop switches the electrodes along it on and off in turn, and the droplet rides along. Routes are how you move, mix and park droplets without clicking every step.

From here on the pictures are simulations you can step through: the green shape is a simulated droplet drawn over a photo of the real chip, and the lit electrodes, routes and phases follow MicroDrop's own rules.

Draw a route Device Viewer, Mode buttons

Press on the droplet's electrode and drag through neighbouring electrodes to where it should go. The run below uses Length 2 and Overlay 1 with Rot Lock on, as the panel starts: the droplet keeps lying across as it turns the corner.

  1. Choose Draw mode (D).
  2. Press on the droplet's first electrode (here 22) and drag through the electrodes you want, in order. Each neighbour you cross adds a step; the line turns yellow because it is the selected route.
  3. Let go at the end. The route appears in the Path / Run table under Paths, named like Path: 22 → 79.
  4. Click Run selected routes to run it now. The tinted electrodes show the whole area the droplet will sweep, and a dashed outline shows where it is at each moment.

Auto route Device Viewer, Mode buttons

Let MicroDrop find the path for you.

  1. Choose Autoroute (A).
  2. Press on the start electrode and drag towards the destination. A pink preview shows the shortest path around your other routes; it follows the pointer.
  3. Let go on the destination. The preview becomes a normal route. Hold Alt while dragging to allow it to cross existing routes.

Loops: mixing in place Device Viewer

Drag a route back onto the electrode where it started and it becomes a loop. A droplet sent round a loop again and again mixes its contents, which a droplet sitting still does very slowly.

  • The Path table names it Loop @ 40. Unselected loops are drawn orange or red, so you can tell which way they turn.
  • Set how many laps with Reps, or how long to keep going with Rep Duration (below).
  • Give the droplet room to go round: here a droplet two electrodes long circles a block of 2×3. With Rot Lock on (as the panel starts) it keeps lying the way it set off; the simulator below shows the difference.
  • Several routes can run at once, one per droplet: each gets its own tint, so you can mix several droplets side by side.

Execution Settings Paths ▸ Execution Settings

These shape how the selected route runs. Open Paths ▸ Execution Settings in the side panel. Each run is a list of phases: one set of electrodes switched on for one Duration.

SettingWhat it does
DurationSeconds each phase is held before the next one (1.0 to start with).
LengthHow many electrodes along the route are on at once: the droplet's footprint along its way.
OverlayHow many of those stay on into the next phase, so each phase moves on Length − Overlay electrodes. At most Length − 1; where the footprint could not move without a gap, MicroDrop uses the smallest overlay that keeps it continuous.
RepsLaps of a loop, after which the droplet returns onto the loop's start. With Lin Reps it replays an open route instead. Greyed out (kept at 1) when there is no loop and Lin Reps is off.
Rep DurationInstead of Reps, for a loop: as many whole laps as fit in this many seconds (at least one), then the droplet waits at the start until the time is up. Editing Reps sets it back to 0, and the other way round.
Ramp UpStart gently: the footprint comes on a row at a time from its tail (one electrode at a time for a one-wide route) until it is full.
Ramp DnFinish gently: at the end the footprint goes off a row at a time from its tail, down to the head row.
Lin RepsReplay an open route Reps times, each time starting again from its first electrode (not back and forth).
LanesExtra electrodes beside the route: the left number adds lanes on one side, the right number on the other, up to 4 in all. With Rot Lock on they are the screen-left and screen-right of travel; with it off and In/Out ticked, inside and outside of the turns. Where a lane runs off the electrodes, the nearest free electrodes make up the count, so a wall shifts the footprint sideways.
Rot LockOn (as it starts): the footprint keeps the orientation it set off with and only slides through corners. Off: it turns with the route, re-hung behind the leading electrode after every corner. A square footprint (Length equal to its width) always just slides.
Re-centreShown with Rot Lock on: when the locked footprint moves sideways to its own direction, it is centred on the route. Off, it keeps the position it arrived in, so the route runs along its edge.
In/OutShown with Rot Lock off: read the two lane numbers as inside / outside of the turn instead of left / right.

Changes show at once in the tinted preview on the chip (the Route Shape: every electrode the run will use). To store them in a protocol step, click Apply execution params to step (see Design a protocol).

7. Split a droplet

Hold the part you want to keep where it is and pull the rest away: the droplet tears in one piece. The quickest split, the one in the clip, takes one electrode's worth off a larger droplet in a few seconds.

  1. Start with a droplet bigger than the one you want, for example four electrodes' worth in a row on 22, 16, 103 and 97.
  2. Leave on the electrode the new droplet should sit on (97; ① in the photo) and switch off its neighbour (103; ②).
  3. At the same time switch on two electrodes beside the far end (24 and 25, above 22 and 16; ③). The rest of the droplet is pulled away from 97, and a neck forms over 103.
  4. The neck thins and snaps: 97 keeps exactly one electrode's worth, and the other three electrodes' worth gathers on 22, 16, 24 and 25. If the neck lingers, switch on one more electrode further away from 97. A tiny bead of liquid can be left between the two droplets; move one of them over it and it is picked up again.
  5. The new droplet on 97 is a droplet like any other: move it by switching on the electrode next to it and then switching off the one it sits on, as in chapter 5.
To halve a droplet instead, pull its two halves apart: switch on the electrodes beyond both ends and switch off the middle ones; the middle thins and snaps. With the keyboard: switch on the droplet's electrodes, hold Ctrl+Shift and press → (or ↓) repeatedly to push them apart from the middle; ←/↑ brings them back. A droplet keeps its volume: a split shares it out, a merge adds it up, and a droplet on a single electrode is too small to split.

8. Design a protocol

Everything so far you did by hand. A protocol writes it down as a list of steps, so MicroDrop can repeat it exactly, as often as you like.

What a step is Pluggable Protocol Tree

A step is one line in the protocol: which electrodes are on, which routes run, at what voltage and frequency, and for how long. When the protocol runs, MicroDrop does the steps from top to bottom.

  • Select a step in the tree, and the Device Viewer shows that step: its electrodes and routes. The status bar reads Editing: Step 1.
  • Click electrodes or draw routes while a step is selected, and they are written straight into that step's Electrodes and Routes cells.
  • Execution Settings are the exception: they are saved into the step only when you click . If you forget, MicroDrop asks Commit or Discard when you move to another step.
  • Press Esc in the tree to deselect: you are back in Free Mode, where clicks are not saved into any step. Free-mode changes can later be kept with Insert as New Step.

Build a sequence by clicking protocol pane and Device Viewer

The quickest way to write moves down: one step per move, each a copy of the one before with one electrode changed.

  1. In an empty protocol, click Add step below selection. A step appears and is selected; the status bar reads Editing: Step 1.
  2. Click the electrodes under the droplet. They are written into the step (its Electrodes count goes up).
  3. Click Next Step (S). On the last step it adds a copy of that step and selects the copy.
  4. In the copy, click the electrode the droplet should move onto, and click the one it leaves to switch it off. Repeat and the two clicks for every move.
  5. To look through what you wrote, click or drag along the timeline under Experiment: each step is selected in turn and the Device Viewer shows it.

Fill a column: ramps and alternating values protocol pane ▸ Fill a column

To give a run of steps a pattern, such as a voltage that climbs or alternates, fill the column in one go instead of editing every cell.

  1. Select the steps to fill: click the first, then Shift-click the last.
  2. Click (④ in the toolbar picture), or right-click ▸ Fill Pattern…, or press Ctrl+Shift+F.
  3. In Column, pick the setting to fill, here Voltage (V).
  4. Pick a Pattern. Ramp counts from From to To across the steps. Alternate cycles the Values you type, separated by commas or spaces, for example 120, 125.
  5. Read the line under the values: it says what will happen, for example Will set 9 selected steps, cycling 120, 125. Values are rounded to whole numbers. A value outside the column's range (30 to 140 V for voltage) shows a message instead, and OK stays greyed out until it is fixed.
  6. Click OK.

With one step selected, Fill Pattern adds new steps after it instead (copies of it, as many as New steps); with none selected, it adds them after the last step.

Worked example: load, dispense, move, mix, split your first protocol

Build this protocol step by step. Each row is one step; the picture plays it back. Click Step ▸ to follow along.

  1. Start fresh. Click New protocol. You get one step called Step. Double-click its Name and type Load.
  2. Load. With Load selected, click reservoir 26 in the Device Viewer (Electrodes becomes 1). Set Duration to 5. In Message type the pipetting instruction.
  3. Dispense. Click , name it Dispense, and click 26, 20, 21, 22, 16. Duration 3.
  4. Cut. Add Cut with 26, 22, 16 on (the neck electrodes 20 and 21 off). Duration 2.
  5. Move. Add Move. In Draw mode drag a route from 22 through 16, 103, then up 94, 90, 86 to 79. In Execution Settings set Duration 0.7, Length 2, Overlay 1 (leave Rot Lock on), then click .
  6. Mix. Add Mix and draw a loop from 40 right through 79 to 81, up to 78, back along 77 and 42, and down onto 40. Same Length 2, Overlay 1, click . Set its Route Reps to 3 for three laps.
  7. Stretch and Split. The loop leaves the droplet on 40 and 79. Add Stretch with 38, 40, 79, 81 on, then Split with only 38 and 81 on.
  8. Save. Click (Ctrl+S) and give it a name. Protocols are saved as .json files, by default in a folder named after this chip inside MicroDrop's Protocols folder. Open them again with , or File ▸ Protocol ▸ Load.
The title of the pane shows [modified] while there are unsaved changes. Changes live in memory until you save; MicroDrop asks before throwing them away.

Experiment, timers and timeline under the transport buttons

  • Experiment: the folder where this session's data goes (captures, logs and a copy of every protocol you run). Click the name to open the folder; starts a new experiment and adds a note to it.
  • The bar under it is the timeline: one cell per step. Click a cell, or drag along the bar, to go to that step. When nothing is running this just selects the step, and the Device Viewer shows it; while paused, the run continues from there.
  • Protocol, Step and Phase are timers: the first number is running time, the one in brackets the clock time including pauses.
  • Show full timeline: show every repetition of every step in the timeline, instead of one cell per step.

Phases and phase navigation protocol pane and Device Viewer ▸ Paths

A step with routes runs as a series of phases: each phase is one set of switched-on electrodes held for one Duration, like the frames of the pictures above. The Move step of the example has six phases; one lap of Mix has six, and after the last lap one more puts the droplet back on its start. The simulator under Execution Settings shows the phases for any setting.

  • Before running, check a step move by move: select it and tick Phase navigation. and appear beside Play (Ctrl+← / Ctrl+→). With Realtime on, the DropBot follows each phase, so you can walk a droplet through slowly.
  • The same switch sits in the Device Viewer: the Phases checkbox under Paths, with / and a counter like Phase: 3/5. The two are kept in step with each other.
  • While paused, the same previous / next phase buttons appear around Resume, so you can back up a move and try again.

Run it your first run

  1. Load the protocol, check Connection: Active, and select the first step.
  2. Click Play Protocol. If Realtime is off, MicroDrop switches it on for the run and off again afterwards. If it is already on, MicroDrop asks Keep Realtime Mode Enabled Post-Protocol?: Yes leaves it on after the run, No switches it off. Tick Don't ask again to stop the question (it can be changed in preferences).
  3. The running step is highlighted in the tree and the Device Viewer follows it. At Load the message appears: pipette, then click Continue.
  4. Watch the droplet. If something goes wrong, pause, step back a phase, and resume; or stop.
  5. At the end all electrodes switch off. MicroDrop asks Create New Experiment?: Yes starts a fresh experiment folder for your next run.
  6. A run summary is written: Run Summary Generated names the report file, a web page in the experiment's reports folder. Click the name to open it, or open old ones later with Browse session reports.

Glossary

Electrode
One of the metal pads on the chip. Switching it on pulls liquid onto it.
Reservoir
A large electrode at the side of the chip that holds a supply of liquid.
Top plate
The glass plate over the electrodes. Liquid moves in the gap underneath it.
Realtime
When on, the DropBot powers electrodes the moment you click them.
Route
A line drawn across electrodes for a droplet to follow. A route that returns to its start is a loop.
Phase
One moment of a route: the electrodes switched on together for one Duration.
Step
One row of a protocol: electrodes, routes, voltage and timing.
Free Mode
No step selected: clicks are tried out, not saved into the protocol.
