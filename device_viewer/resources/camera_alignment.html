Camera & Alignment

MicroDrop can show a live camera picture of the chip under the electrode outlines, so you click right on the liquid you see. This tutorial covers the Camera Controls section of the Device Viewer side panel: choosing a camera, taking pictures and videos, and lining the camera picture up with the chip drawing.

The short clips are real screen recordings; they play when they scroll into view. The interactive figures let you try alignment without a camera.

Overview

Alignment tells MicroDrop where the chip sits in the camera picture. You do the careful part once per chip design: mark four corners on a camera frame and the same four corners on the drawing. MicroDrop saves the drawing's corners as the device's endpoint, so every later alignment of that device is a few drags and one click.

Two tools move the camera picture:

  • Align Camera opens the Camera Alignment window: a camera frame and the chip drawing side by side, each with four numbered dots. This is the main workflow and the one the walkthrough follows.
  • Align Camera Perspective puts handles straight on the Device Viewer, for touching up by eye. See Fine-tune on the device view.

Pictures and videos go into the current experiment's folder; see Where files go.

The camera section sits in the side panel to the right of the Device Viewer. If the panel is hidden, click the strip at the picture's right edge; click the Camera Controls heading to fold the section open.

Quick walkthrough

From a camera that is plugged in to a picture that lines up with the electrodes. The first alignment of a chip design takes a few minutes; later ones take seconds.

  1. 1Pick the camera and switch it on

    In Camera Controls, choose your camera in Camera. Not listed? Plug it in and click Refresh Camera List.

    Choose a Resolution. Each entry reads like FHD (1920x1080) [NV12] @ 30 fps: size, pixel format and frame rate. Full HD (FHD) is a good start; larger sizes cost speed (see Resolution).

    Click to switch the camera on. The button turns to and the live picture appears under the outlines.

  2. 2Open Camera Alignment and show the corners

    Click Align Camera. The Camera Alignment window opens with a still frame from the camera on the left (Device Outline) and the chip drawing on the right (Endpoint — device name).

    Above the camera frame, click Show every corner the dots can snap onto. Small cyan marks appear on the corners MicroDrop found in the frame.

    Black frame and an orange "No camera frame" message? The camera was off; see When something goes wrong.

  3. 3Put the four camera dots on the chip

    Drag the four orange dots onto four corners of the electrode area in the camera frame. Pick corners you can also find on the drawing, such as the outer corners of the electrode grid.

    A dot dropped near a cyan mark jumps onto it (Snap radius). Scroll the mouse wheel to zoom in for precision, and drag the picture (not a dot) to pan. fits the whole frame again.

    The dots are numbered 1 to 4. Remember which corner has which number: the drawing's dots must follow the same order.

  4. 4Turn both sides the same way

    Make the camera frame and the drawing face the same way, so the dots are easy to match. Each side has Rotate a quarter turn clockwise; the camera side also has Mirror the camera image left–right and top–bottom. The dots move with the picture.

    Want to turn only the dots and leave the picture still? Switch on the image lock first (it shows while on).

    Turning and mirroring here only change how the two sides are shown, so you can compare them. They are not saved; what counts is which dot sits on which corner. Try it in the orientation puzzle.

  5. 5Place the drawing's dots: the endpoint

    On the right, drag the drawing's four dots onto the same four corners you used in the camera frame, with the same numbers: dot 1 on the same corner in both panes, and so on.

    Hovering or pressing a dot highlights the dot with the same number in the other pane, so you can check the pairs.

    These four drawing positions are the device's endpoint. Once saved, they are kept for this chip design, and the window opens with them next time.

  6. 6Confirm, then check

    Click the green Confirm Alignment. MicroDrop saves the endpoint (the status bar says Saved camera-alignment endpoint for device), then glides the camera picture so the four camera corners land on the four drawing corners.

    The window stays open so you can adjust and confirm again. Close it with its × when done.

    Check that the outlines sit on the real electrode edges everywhere. If an edge is off, open Align Camera again and nudge the dots, or touch up on the device view.

  7. 7Take a picture or a video

    Click Capture Image to save the camera picture as the Device Viewer shows it, straightened by the alignment (without the electrode outlines), as a PNG.

    Click Start Recording Video to start a video; the button stays highlighted while it records. Click it again to stop.

    Both work with the camera off too: MicroDrop switches it on for the shot and off again afterwards. The status bar shows Image Captured: or Video Captured: with a link to the file for a few seconds. Where files go lists the folders.

Next time: the same chip design

MicroDrop remembers the alignment between sessions, so if neither camera nor chip holder moved, the picture still lines up after a restart. When something did move:

  1. Click Align Camera. The drawing side opens with the saved endpoint already in place.
  2. Drag the camera dots onto the same four corners in the frame. The window grabs a fresh frame when it opens; grabs another if the chip moved since.
  3. Click Confirm Alignment. No need to touch the drawing side.

Go To Endpoint in Camera Controls repeats only the last part: it glides the corner points currently marked on the camera picture onto the saved endpoint. Use it after dragging the handles on the device view by hand, to snap them back, or after staging new camera points with in the Device Outline pane.

Fine-tune on the device view

For small corrections you can drag the camera picture's corners right on the Device Viewer, without the alignment window.

  1. Click Align Camera Perspective. If the picture is already aligned, the button turns green and a red frame shows the four marked points. The status bar says Drag vertices to align with device outline.
  2. Drag a red corner. The camera picture stretches so that point moves with it; the electrode outlines stay put.
  3. Click the button again to finish.

Nothing aligned yet? Then the status bar says Select 4 points on image: click four spots on the camera picture, such as the chip's corners. After the fourth the red frame appears and you drag its corners onto the matching places of the outlines.

Right-click the view while the handles are shown for two more choices: Reset Reference Rectangle (start again by clicking four new points) and Edit Reference Rect (tooltip: Edit Reference Rectangle without changing camera perspective), which moves the red points without moving the picture, for when you want to pick better reference spots first.

Alignment terms

What the words in the alignment window mean, and the values MicroDrop starts with.

Perspective correction (homography) what alignment computes

The stretch that turns the camera's slanted view of the chip into a straight, top-down picture that fits the drawing.

What it is

Four point pairs (a camera spot and the drawing spot it belongs on) fix one "projective" stretch: it can move, turn, scale and tilt the picture, and keeps straight lines straight. Mathematicians call it a homography.

When to use it

Every alignment computes one; you never type numbers.

How to read it

Good alignment: outlines sit on the real electrode edges across the whole chip, not just near the dots. Pick dots far apart (the outer corners) for the best result.

Typical value

Kept between sessions; reset with Reset Camera Perspective.

Reference grid orange frame in both panes

The orange four-sided frame joining the four dots; the image lock's tooltip calls it the reference grid.

What it is

A visual guide: it shows the shape the four dots make, so a crossed or twisted order is easy to spot.

How to read it

A clean four-sided shape in both panes means the dots go round the same way. A bow-tie shape means two dots are swapped.

Typical value

Orange (#ffa000), 3 px wide; see Frame width.

Numbered dots (1–4) both panes

The four handles you drag. Dot 1 in the camera frame belongs on the same corner as dot 1 on the drawing.

What it is

A fresh set starts numbered from the top-left, clockwise: 1 top-left, 2 top-right, 3 bottom-right, 4 bottom-left. On the drawing side the numbers are tidied up the same way when the endpoint is saved.

When to use it

Always: the pairs are what the alignment is made of.

How to read it

The number sits beside each dot, in the dot's colour. Hover or press a dot: the dot with the same number lights up in the other pane too. If you turn or mirror the camera frame, its dots move with it, so dot 1 may no longer be top-left; that is fine as long as both panes agree.

Typical value

When the orders differ, Confirm Alignment asks before going on (see When something goes wrong).

Snap corners eye button above each pane

The spots a dot jumps onto when you let go of it nearby.

What it is

On the camera side, corners MicroDrop finds in the frame by looking for sharp light/dark corners. On the drawing side, every corner of every electrode outline.

When to use it

Leave them on while placing dots; switch the cyan marks off with the eye button if they clutter the view. Snapping works whether the marks are shown or not.

How to read it

Cyan marks. Dirt, bubbles and the chip's edge make corners too; zoom in and check the dot landed on the corner you meant. Recapture finds them again on a fresh frame.

Typical value

Marks hidden when the window opens.

Image lock lock button above each pane

Keeps the picture still while Rotate (and, on the camera side, Mirror) moves only the dots.

What it is

With the lock on, rotating turns the four dots about their middle; the picture, its snap corners and its orientation stay as they are.

When to use it

When the picture already faces the right way but your dots went round in the wrong order: one or more quarter turns renumber them without dragging.

How to read it

highlighted: locked. : picture and dots move together.

Typical value

Off. Greyed out until the pane has a picture. Not saved: closing the window forgets it, and so does the camera side's reset button.

Endpoint Endpoint pane

Where the four camera points belong on the drawing, saved once per device (chip design).

What it is

Four positions on the device drawing. MicroDrop keeps one set per device, under the device file's name, in camera_endpoints.json (see Where files go).

When to use it

Set it carefully the first time you use a chip design; afterwards leave it alone unless it is wrong.

How to read it

The drawing side's title names the device: Endpoint — 90_pin_array. With no saved endpoint, the dots start just inside the drawing's edges.

Typical value

None until you save one. Saved by Confirm Alignment or the drawing side's ; deleted by its .

Outline points Device Outline pane

The four camera dots: where the chosen corners are in this camera frame.

What it is

Positions in the camera picture, not saved per device: they change whenever the camera or chip moves. Confirming (or the camera side's ) marks them on the Device Viewer as the start points of the next glide.

When to use it

Every alignment.

Typical value

A fresh window starts them where the current alignment has them, or on a grid inside the frame.

Confirm Alignment bottom of the alignment window

The one button that applies everything.

What it is

In order: saves the endpoint, marks the outline points on the Device Viewer, then runs Go To Endpoint.

When to use it

After placing both sets of dots. Press it again after any correction.

How to read it

Greyed out while the camera side has no frame. Asks first if the two dot orders differ.

Typical value

The window stays open afterwards; close it yourself.

Go To Endpoint Camera Controls ▸ Go To Endpoint

Glides the camera picture so the marked camera points land on the saved endpoint.

What it is

Moves the four marked points smoothly onto the endpoint over about a second and a half, stretching the picture as it goes, then returns to the mode you were in.

When to use it

Runs by itself after Confirm Alignment. Click it yourself after dragging the red handles on the device view, or after staging outline points with the camera side's .

How to read it

Needs a saved endpoint and four marked points; otherwise it says which is missing.

Rotate Camera vs Rotate Device Camera Controls, keys R and Shift+R

Two different quarter turns on the Device Viewer.

What it is

Rotate Camera (Shift+R) turns only the camera picture about its centre; the outlines stay. Rotate Device (R) turns the whole view, outlines and picture together, a quarter turn clockwise.

When to use it

Rotate Camera when the picture is turned against the drawing (it changes the alignment). Rotate Device when you just want to look at the chip another way round; the alignment is untouched.

How to read it

Both are remembered for next time. The keys work with the Device Viewer selected; F1 lists all its keys.

Typical value

Not rotated.

Orientation puzzle Device Outline: rotate and mirror

A camera can show the chip turned or mirrored compared with the drawing. Practise undoing that with the window's buttons.

Overlay Settings

The sidebar the chevron at the alignment window's right edge opens. It changes how the dots, frame and markers look and how far dots snap. Changes apply at once to both panes and are saved with your preferences; Reset to Defaults puts every value back. These settings appear only here, not in Edit ▸ Preferences.

Snap radius Overlay Settings ▸ Snap radius (px)

How close to a snap corner you must let go of a dot for it to jump there.

What it is

A distance in screen pixels, so it covers less of the chip the further you zoom in.

When to use it

Lower it if dots keep jumping to the wrong corner; set 0 to switch snapping off. Raise it on a large screen.

Typical value

100 px, from 0 to 200. Try it in the alignment demo.

Dot radius Overlay Settings ▸ Dot radius (px)

The size of the four draggable dots.

When to use it

Bigger for a touchscreen, smaller to see the corner under the dot.

How to read it

The dot numbers grow with it.

Typical value

8 px, from 2 to 40.

Frame width Overlay Settings ▸ Frame width (px)

The line width of the orange reference grid.

Typical value

3 px, from 1 to 20.

Corner marker size and alpha Overlay Settings ▸ Corner marker size (px), Corner marker alpha

How big and how see-through the cyan snap-corner marks are.

What it is

Size in screen pixels, the same at any zoom. Alpha is opacity: 0 invisible, 1 solid.

When to use it

Shrink or fade them when a busy frame is covered in marks.

Typical value

Size 6 px (1 to 30); alpha 0.6 (0 to 1).

Highlight colour, alpha and size Overlay Settings ▸ Highlight color, Highlight alpha, Highlight size (x dot)

The ring that marks the dot under the pointer, and its partner in the other pane.

What it is

A ring with a soft halo. Size is a multiple of the dot radius.

When to use it

Make it bolder if you lose track of which dots pair up.

Typical value

Colour the same as the dots (#ff6400), alpha 1.0, size 1.7 × dot (1 to 4).

Number alpha Overlay Settings ▸ Number alpha

How visible the 1–4 numbers beside the dots are.

How to read it

0 hides the numbers; 1 is solid.

Typical value

1.0.

Frame, dot, dot ring and corner marker colours Overlay Settings ▸ Frame color, Dot color, Dot ring color, Corner marker color

Colour wells for each part of the overlay.

When to use it

Change a colour that disappears against your chip or lighting, for example orange on a copper-coloured board.

Typical value

Frame orange #ffa000, dot #ff6400, dot ring white #ffffff, corner marker cyan #00e5ff.

Camera, capture and video settings

The camera choices in Camera Controls and the two groups under Edit ▸ Preferences ▸ Video Settings.

Camera Camera Controls ▸ Camera

Which camera the Device Viewer shows.

What it is

The cameras the computer reports, then cameras added by plugins (for example a fluorescence camera), then <No Camera>.

When to use it

Pick <No Camera> to run without a picture; the camera, record, capture and Rotate Camera buttons grey out.

How to read it

Plugin cameras cannot be recorded with Start Recording Video; the button greys out for them.

Typical value

The camera you used last time. If it is missing at start-up, the first one listed.

Resolution Camera Controls ▸ Resolution

The picture size, pixel format and frame rate the camera delivers.

What it is

Common sizes by name: SD (640x480), HD (1280x720), FHD (1920x1080), 2K (2048x1080), QHD (2560x1440), 4K (3840x2160), 4K DCI (4096x2160). The last entry, Show all resolutions…, lists every size the camera offers; Show common resolutions… goes back.

When to use it

Bigger shows more detail but makes streaming, recording and analysis heavier. Recording needs at least 20 fps: pick a size with a higher rate if Recording Not Supported appears.

How to read it

Above 1920x1080 MicroDrop warns once (High resolution selected); tick Don't show this again to stop it, or untick Hide High Resolution Warning in Video Settings to bring it back.

Typical value

Your last choice; the first time, the middle entry of the list.

Camera Properties Camera Controls ▸ Camera Properties

A separate window with the selected camera's own controls.

What it is

A Focus slider (Near to Far), with Auto and a one-shot autofocus button where the camera supports them, and one slider per image control the camera has (exposure, brightness, contrast, …) with Defaults.

When to use it

To sharpen or brighten the picture before aligning or capturing.

How to read it

The button is hidden for cameras that offer none of these controls.

Preferred Video Format Video Settings ▸ Video Format

The pixel format MicroDrop asks the camera for.

What it is

NV12 (raw, uncompressed colour) or JPEG (compressed by the camera). With Strictly Use Only Preferred Video Format? ticked, the Resolution list offers only that format.

When to use it

Switch to JPEG if a USB camera only reaches high resolutions or frame rates in JPEG.

How to read it

If the camera has no format of that kind, MicroDrop says Preferred format NV12 not supported. (or JPEG) and lists the others instead.

Typical value

NV12 on Windows and macOS, JPEG on Linux; strict on.

Recording Engine Video Settings ▸ Video Recording ▸ Recording Engine

Which program turns camera frames into a video file.

What it is

Qt MediaRecorder (hardware): the computer's built-in, hardware-accelerated encoder, lightest on the processor. FFmpeg process: frames handed to a separate ffmpeg program, with full control over the encoder at a higher processor cost.

When to use it

Stay on Qt unless you need FFmpeg's codec or quality controls.

Typical value

Qt MediaRecorder (hardware). Changes apply from the next recording; no restart.

Qt: Video Format, Video Codec, MKV Quality Video Recording ▸ Qt Recording

Settings of the Qt recorder.

What it is

Video Format: MP4 plays everywhere, but a crash while recording can leave the file unreadable; MKV is written as it goes and survives a crash. Video Codec: the codecs this computer can encode into that format; H.264 plays most widely. MKV Quality (MKV only): a tier per resolution class.

How to read it

Each tier shows its bit rate and size per minute. At record time the class matching the camera's real size and frame rate applies.

Typical value

MP4, H.264, tiers on Auto (same as Medium).

FFmpeg: Container, Video Codec, Preset, Quality (CRF) Video Recording ▸ FFmpeg Recording

Settings of the FFmpeg recorder.

What it is

Container: mkv stays recoverable after a crash; mp4 is most widely supported but corrupts if not closed cleanly. Video Codec: libx264 (H.264, fastest, plays everywhere) or libx265 (H.265, about half the size, more processor, fewer players). Preset: ultrafast … veryslow; faster presets use less processor but make bigger files at the same quality. Quality (CRF): 0 lossless to 51 worst; lower is bigger. Extra Output Args: extra ffmpeg options for experts.

When to use it

Raise CRF a few steps for long recordings where size matters more than detail.

How to read it

Frame rate, size and pixel format always come from the camera.

Typical value

mkv, libx264, ultrafast, CRF 17 (about visually lossless).

Caveats

  • One endpoint per device file name. Two chip designs saved under the same file name share an endpoint; renaming an SVG loses it.
  • The current alignment is shared by all devices. After switching to another chip design, run Align Camera (or Go To Endpoint) for it.
  • Turning or mirroring in the alignment window is not saved; only the dot positions are.
  • Reset Camera Perspective removes the alignment, not the endpoint; the endpoint's own reset button removes the endpoint.
  • One recording at a time. Plugin cameras (for example a fluorescence camera) cannot be recorded here, and some resolutions are too slow to record.
  • MP4 recordings can be lost in a crash; choose MKV if the computer is unstable.

Glossary

Alignment
Stretching the camera picture so the chip in it lies under the electrode outlines.
Device / SVG
The drawing of a chip design's electrodes, loaded from an .svg file.
Endpoint
Four saved positions on a device drawing that the camera points glide onto.
Homography
The stretch that maps one four-sided shape onto another and keeps straight lines straight.
Snap
A dot jumping onto a nearby corner when you let go.
Frame rate (fps)
Pictures per second the camera delivers.
Codec
The method that compresses video, such as H.264.
Container
The video file type that holds the compressed video: MP4 or MKV.
CRF
Constant Rate Factor, FFmpeg's quality number: lower means better and bigger.
Bit rate (Mbps)
Millions of bits of video per second; 8 Mbps is about 60 MB per minute.
UTC
Coordinated Universal Time, used in file names so they sort the same everywhere.
